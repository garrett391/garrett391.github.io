Shed

A card game everyone in my family plays with different rules, rebuilt so the rules are data instead of code. The interesting part isn't the game — it's the engine underneath, which is pure, serialisable, and tested harder than the app around it.

Why it exists

Shed — Palace, Karma, a few other names — is trivial to play and genuinely hard to teach. Every group has its own house rules and every group is certain theirs are the real ones. I wanted a version that could hold all of them at once, and a way to settle arguments without anyone reciting rules from memory.

The engine is pure, and that's the whole design

All game logic lives in src/engine/ and imports nothing — no React, no storage, no I/O. The core call is playCards(state, playerIdx, cardIds), which returns a new state plus an array of events and mutates nothing.

Three things fall out of that. Every rule becomes testable in isolation. The bot consumes exactly the same GameState the UI does, so it can be swapped for a search algorithm or a remote player without the engine noticing. And because the state is serialisable, the same engine that runs on the phone today could run authoritatively on a server tomorrow — clients would send intent and render whatever state came back.

A Zustand store is the only mutable thing in the app. It wraps the engine and holds the UI-only concerns: which cards are selected, whether the current player has revealed their hand past the pass-the-device privacy gate, and the last batch of events for the in-game banner.

Rules as data

The full ruleset is in there — meets-or-beats, the power cards (2 resets, 7 forces the next play low, 8 is invisible, 10 burns the pile), four-of-a-kind burns with 8s in the middle still counting, and the three phases from hand to face-up to a blind face-down flip.

On top of that sit six named presets — Justin's Schism, Super Tens, Chaos Shed, OFCOM Standard, the Backpacker's Codex and one more — that reconfigure the engine rather than branch through it. The app generates a human-readable diff of whatever variant is active, so nobody has to remember what they agreed to.

Testing is where the work went

57 unit tests cover every power card, every variant, and the edge cases that actually bite: an 8 inside a four-of-a-kind, a 10 played on a 7, a failed face-down flip, and who starts when nobody holds a non-power card.

Underneath those is a fuzz harness that plays thousands of random games across every preset and asserts two invariants: cards are never created or destroyed, and every game terminates. Those are the two properties that make a card game engine trustworthy, and neither is something you can reach by writing more example tests.

The bot

Roughly fifty lines of heuristics, deliberately small and self-contained. It plays a reasonable game and loses to anyone paying attention, which is the correct difficulty for teaching someone the rules. The interface is narrow enough that replacing it with a real search is an afternoon rather than a refactor.

What's deliberately missing

No online multiplayer, no accounts, no stats or leaderboards, no spectator mode. The engine was built so those stay cheap to add later — that's the reason it's pure — but none of them make the game better at a kitchen table, which is the only place it gets played.
